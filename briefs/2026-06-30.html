Today's Thesis

The Best Quarter Since 2020 Is in the Books. The Hard Part Starts Tomorrow.

The S&P 500 and Nasdaq closed Q2 2026 with their best quarterly performance since 2020 — the year stocks bounced back from the COVID collapse — finishing today at 7,499 and 26,214 respectively. What actually happened across Q2 was the market pricing every major open question toward its optimistic resolution at the same time: AI hardware demand is genuine, the Iran war is contained and not disrupting oil supply, and a more permissive regulatory environment means higher corporate profits. Today the Supreme Court added one more brick to that structure by rolling back all remaining federal campaign spending limits, its second consecutive governance-reshaping ruling in two days. The quarter looks like a victory lap — the more accurate description is a very large, very coherent bet that has not yet been graded. Q3 earnings season, arriving in mid-July, is the first test.

What's Actually Driving This

A historically strong quarter-end close and a second consecutive Supreme Court governance ruling define the session

QUARTER-END RECORD
Q2 2026 closes as the best quarter for stocks since 2020 — a six-year high-water mark reached during an active war
Today is the final session of Q2 2026, and the tally is historically significant: the S&P 500 and Nasdaq posted their strongest quarter since Q2 2020 — the pandemic bounce-back — while a military conflict was active in the Middle East. The quarter's performance is grounded in a coherent thesis: AI hardware demand is documented with chipmakers reportedly tripling in H1 2026, corporate earnings are growing, the Iran conflict has not disrupted oil supply, and the regulatory environment is shifting toward permissiveness. Today's modest session gain also carries one mechanical factor worth noting: quarter-end window dressing — the standard practice where professional fund managers buy the period's winning stocks in the final days of the quarter so their reports show they held them — provides a small artificial lift that typically fades in the first few sessions of the new quarter.
The record is real and grounded in measurable demand data — this was not a speculative bubble quarter. But the bar for Q3 is now set at a record high: if mid-July earnings from major AI platform companies don't show AI product revenues growing in proportion to AI infrastructure spending, markets will begin repricing a thesis that has already been fully endorsed at these levels.
CAMPAIGN FINANCE RULING
The Supreme Court eliminated all remaining federal caps on political campaign spending — its second major governance ruling in two consecutive days
In a ruling separate from yesterday's agency-independence decision, the Court held that federal limits on campaign spending violate the First Amendment's guarantee of free speech. The practical consequence: there is now no legally enforceable federal ceiling on how much money — including corporate money — can flow into political campaigns. This extends the Court's 2010 Citizens United decision, which first opened the door to unlimited outside political spending through so-called super PACs (independent political action committees that can raise and spend without legal caps) and removes additional constraints that had survived that earlier ruling. For markets, the immediate read is pro-business continuity: the political coalition currently controlling the regulatory agenda just received a stronger financial infrastructure to maintain that control.
The ruling moves no stock today, which is the correct market reaction. Watch whether any major technology, financial, or energy company announces a new political spending program within the next 30 days — that would be the first concrete signal of which industries intend to immediately use the new legal environment to shape their regulatory futures.
The Core Dynamic

Q2 was the vote. Q3 is when the market finds out whether it voted correctly.

Warren Buffett's mentor Benjamin Graham wrote that in the short run, the market is a voting machine — it reflects what investors believe and want to be true. In the long run, it is a weighing machine — it reflects what companies actually earn. Q2 2026 was a quarter of aggressive, internally consistent voting: for AI, against inflation risk, against geopolitical disruption, for deregulation. Every open question was resolved toward the optimistic outcome, and the scoreboard reflects it. The danger is not that any of those bets were wrong — they may well prove correct — it is that the market has already been paid for being right, leaving no buffer for the scenarios that remain genuinely open. This moment is specifically harder than a typical record-close scenario because the outstanding questions — AI revenue materialization, Fed independence, the Iran war's slow-burn economic effects — are all converging in the same narrow summer window, and a miss on any single one of them lands against a market that has priced them all as wins simultaneously.

Historical Precedent

Record quarters during active conflicts follow a specific pattern — what happens next depends entirely on whether the underlying thesis holds

2003
After the U.S. declared 'Mission Accomplished' in Iraq in May 2003, the Nasdaq gained roughly 21% in Q2 2003 — one of the strongest quarters for technology stocks in years, achieved while an active military conflict continued. The market was correct: the war did not disrupt domestic economic growth, earnings expanded, oil stayed manageable, and the S&P 500 went on to gain roughly 25% over the following 18 months. The ongoing insurgency that followed the initial invasion never altered the economic thesis because the oil market confirmed the good scenario — supply stayed intact and there was nothing concrete to reprice.
Markets can be right to price out a war's economic impact while the conflict continues — if oil doesn't spike, the rally isn't wishful thinking, it's tracking the right variable.
2010
In January 2010, the Supreme Court's Citizens United decision removed limits on corporate outside political spending for the first time — a structural governance change comparable in scope to today's ruling. Markets barely moved on the day of the decision itself, and the S&P 500 finished 2010 up roughly 13%. But over the following decade, industries that moved fastest to deploy their new political spending power — financial services, energy, telecommunications — navigated the regulatory environment measurably better than those that did not. The consequences took three to five years to show up clearly at the sector level, not the index level.
Supreme Court rulings that expand corporate political power are market-neutral on day one and sector-reshaping over a decade — the race to capitalize starts immediately, but the investor payoff arrives slowly and unevenly across industries.
Directional Read

The single variable that determines whether Q2's record is a launchpad or a ceiling is mid-July earnings — specifically whether Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon report AI product revenues growing faster than their capital expenditures (the money companies spend to build AI infrastructure: data centers, chips, and compute capacity). If AI revenues are outpacing AI spending, the Q2 thesis gets validated and Q3 extends the gains. If companies are spending aggressively on AI build-out while AI-specific product revenues lag, the market faces a question it has been successfully avoiding: how long is it willing to pay for potential that keeps being pushed forward? The answer to that question is what Q3 is actually about.

Scenario A — AI revenue confirms: At least two of the four major AI platform companies report in mid-July that AI-specific product revenue grew faster than AI infrastructure spending in Q2 — the monetization gap closes on the scoreboard and Nasdaq tests 27,500 by Labor Day.
Scenario B — Spend without revenue: Major tech companies report another quarter of heavy AI capital expenditure with no proportionate AI product revenue breakout — Nasdaq gives back 8-10% through August as the gap between what was priced and what was earned becomes impossible to rationalize.
