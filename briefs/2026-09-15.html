Today's Thesis

The Bond Market Already Priced In Tomorrow's Fed Hike

The 10-year Treasury yield — the interest rate the government pays to borrow for a decade — closed at 5.041% today, its highest level in 19 years, one day before the Fed is widely expected to raise its benchmark rate for the first time since 2023. Stocks slipped across the board (S&P -0.45%, Nasdaq -0.78%, Dow -0.63%) because oil-driven inflation and rising government borrowing costs are already tightening financial conditions before the Fed has lifted a finger. That's the tell: markets aren't waiting for tomorrow's vote to act tighter, they're already living in it. The real question isn't whether the Fed hikes — it's whether a tool built to cool an overheating economy can do anything about inflation caused by an oil supply shock instead.

What's Actually Driving This

Treasury yields hit a 19-year high while Wall Street braces for tomorrow's Fed hike.

YIELDS AT 19-YEAR HIGH
The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.041% today, its highest level since 2007, a day before the Fed's decision.
This is the same oil-driven inflation dynamic that broke through 5% a month ago, now pushing further past it even as the Treasury insists its bond buyback program is working as intended. That program is succeeding at its narrow job — soaking up extra government bonds to hold rates down — but it's losing to the bigger force: investors demanding more return to hold long-term government debt while oil supply through the Strait of Hormuz keeps shrinking. That means the bond market is tightening financial conditions on its own, independent of anything the Fed decides tomorrow.
This does not resolve tomorrow. Even if the Fed hikes exactly as priced, the question is whether yields stabilize or keep climbing toward 5.25% — a close above that line means the bond market thinks the Fed is behind this problem, not ahead of it.
FED HIKE NOW FULLY PRICED
Wall Street enters the decision fully expecting the Fed's first rate hike since 2023 — a bet built on oil, not overheating growth.
Economist Mark Zandi's warning that a hike would be a 'serious mistake' cuts to the real tension: raising rates doesn't add a single barrel of oil to the market, so it can't fix inflation coming from a supply shock rather than excess demand. This is noise if the Fed frames the hike as inflation-expectations insurance and stocks shrug it off tomorrow; it's signal if the hike lands and yields keep rising anyway.
Watch the market's reaction in the hours after the decision, not the decision itself — yields easing means the Fed bought credibility, yields still climbing means the market thinks the tool doesn't fit the problem.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed is about to raise rates to fight an inflation problem interest rates can't touch.

Rate hikes work by cooling demand — making mortgages and business borrowing pricier so people spend less. This inflation is coming from the supply side instead — oil that can't get through the Strait of Hormuz — which is why hiking here is closer to turning up the air conditioning to fix a broken window: it's the tool available, not the tool aimed at the actual leak.

Directional Read

The variable that matters all week is simple: does the 10-year Treasury yield keep climbing past today's 5.041%, or does it top out once the Fed actually moves tomorrow? If yields keep rising even after a hike, the market has stopped trusting the Fed's tools to fix oil-driven inflation — a much scarier signal than the hike itself. If yields ease once the 'will they, won't they' uncertainty resolves, this was mostly positioning noise. Hold this thought through the decision: the hike isn't the story, what happens to yields after it is.

Scenario A — Hike Clears The Air: The Fed hikes as expected, frames it as insurance against inflation expectations, and yields ease off their highs because the uncertainty itself was half the pressure.
Scenario B — Hike Confirms Powerlessness: The Fed hikes, yields keep climbing past 5.25% anyway, and stocks fall further as markets conclude the Fed has no tool that actually fixes an oil supply shock.
