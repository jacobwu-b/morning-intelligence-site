Today's Thesis

The AI Sell-Off Just Went Global — And the Fed Meeting Nobody's Betting On Starts Today

Samsung and SK Hynix — the two companies that make most of the world's memory chips — fell more than 10% overnight, dragging the South Korean market to a three-month low and pulling the Nasdaq down 0.82% while the Dow climbed 0.96%. This is the same growth-versus-value rotation we've tracked for four straight sessions, but today it stopped being a US story and became a worldwide one: the fear that AI spending won't pay off fast enough, plus rising Chinese competition, is now repricing chip stocks on two continents at once. Separately, the Fed's two-day meeting opened today, and bond investors are conspicuously refusing to place directional bets ahead of tomorrow's decision — a sign the market genuinely doesn't know whether Warsh leans on last week's oil relief or last month's inflation print.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: Korea's chip crash pulls the AI rotation global. Secondary: the Fed opens its meeting with the bond market refusing to bet.

AI SELL-OFF GOES GLOBAL
The rotation out of AI hype and into cash-generating businesses just crossed the Pacific.
Day 4 update: what changed today is where the pressure came from. For three sessions this was a US story — Apple overtaking Nvidia as the most valuable company, the Nasdaq lagging the Dow. Today Samsung and SK Hynix, the backbone of global memory-chip supply, fell over 10% on the same underlying fear — that AI capital spending won't generate returns fast enough to justify current prices — compounded by worries that Chinese chipmakers are closing the gap.
This is signal, not noise, because it's the same fear showing up independently in a different market with different investors. If US chip names open the week echoing Korea's move rather than shrugging it off, the AI-capex anxiety theme has graduated from a rotation to a re-rating — a much bigger and slower-to-reverse event.
FED MEETING OPENS
Bond investors are walking into the Fed's two-day meeting with no strong opinion either way.
That's unusual — normally the market leans one direction ahead of a decision. Here, last weekend's oil-driven relief and last month's 4.2% core inflation print are pulling in opposite directions, and headlines suggest Warsh is under pressure to sound tough on inflation regardless of the oil news, which is exactly the tension the bond market is refusing to pre-judge.
Tomorrow's statement and press conference resolve this, not today. Watch whether Warsh explicitly references the oil drop as a reason for optimism, or treats it as noise against a stickier inflation backdrop — that single framing choice will move yields more than the hold decision itself.
The Core Dynamic

The AI-anxiety trade just stopped being a US phenomenon.

Money that spent four sessions quietly rotating from US tech into US industrials just found the same worry waiting in Seoul — proof this isn't a quirky domestic preference but a global repricing of who actually profits from the AI buildout.

Directional Read

The variable that matters most this week is whether Wednesday's Fed tone leans on the oil relief or on sticky inflation — because that, combined with whether the Korea chip rout spreads to Nvidia and its peers, determines whether this is a one-week wobble or the start of a real valuation reset in AI names. If Warsh sounds dovish and chip stocks stabilize, today looks like noise. If both go the other way at once, you're looking at the first real crack in the AI trade layered on top of a Fed that still can't cut. Hold this one thought through Wednesday: the Fed can manage rates, but it cannot talk chip valuations back up.

Scenario A — Relief Convergence: Warsh acknowledges the oil drop tomorrow and US chip stocks shrug off Korea's move Thursday — the sell-off stays contained to Asia and reads as a one-day scare.
Scenario B — Compounding Repricing: Warsh holds a hawkish line despite the oil relief and US AI-capex names echo Korea's decline this week — the rotation becomes a broader, harder-to-reverse repricing of tech valuations.
