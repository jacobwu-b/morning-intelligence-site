Today's Thesis

Japan Just Shut Off the World's Cheapest Money

Japan's central bank raised its benchmark interest rate to 1.25% today, the highest in 31 years, closing the book on the world's last source of essentially free borrowed money. For decades investors have borrowed cheap yen and used it to fund bets everywhere else — US tech stocks, emerging markets, you name it — and that funding just got more expensive. US markets barely blinked, with the S&P up 0.17% and the Nasdaq up 0.39%, but the last time Japan pulled this move it took days, not hours, to show its teeth. Layer on a brief smoke scare near Riyadh's airport (quickly cleared) and a new US sanctions law tightening the screws on Russia and Iran, and today's calm looks more like a pause than an all-clear.

What's Actually Driving This

Japan's rate hike ends the era of free money; Iran-linked sanctions escalate even as war rhetoric cools

BOJ ENDS FREE MONEY
Japan just raised rates to a 31-year high, closing off the world's last cheap funding source.
The Bank of Japan hiked its benchmark from 1% to 1.25% today to fight inflation driven by high energy costs — a move described as widely anticipated, but structurally huge: Japan has kept rates near zero for decades even as the rest of the world tightened, making it the funding source for the carry trade (borrowing cheap yen to invest in higher-returning assets elsewhere, including US tech). That funding source is now the second major one, after the Fed, to get more expensive this cycle.
The market barely moved on this today, which is exactly what happened before the last carry-trade unwind — the reaction lagged the hike by days, not hours. Watch the yen and Japanese equities over the coming sessions, not just US indices, for the real signal.
PEACE TALK, WAR ACTIONS
A Riyadh airport scare cleared fast, but sanctions on Russia and Iran just got sharper.
Smoke near Riyadh's airport triggered a warning that Saudi authorities lifted within hours — noise, not signal, with no lasting market impact. But the same day, a new Russia sanctions law handed Trump sweeping new tariff powers, and Turkey revoked the license of Iran's Mellat Bank under US pressure — both real policy tightening that contradicts last week's 'towards an end' rhetoric on the Iran war.
This is signal, not noise: actions like sanctions and bank delicensing carry more information than statements. Trust confirmed tanker traffic and the actual oil price over anyone's comments about where the conflict is headed.
The Core Dynamic

Two Central Banks Are Now Squeezing the Same Stalled Economy

The Fed and the Bank of Japan have both just raised rates, while US data on jobs and housing shows barely any improvement in two years. It's like wringing a towel from both ends at once — there's no give left in growth to absorb the extra squeeze. This is harder than a typical hike because Japan was the last holdout of cheap money; now that anchor is gone too, and the stress will show up first in the most leveraged, most crowded trades — likely the AI-linked tech names currently leading the market.

Historical Precedent

Small Hikes From Cheap-Money Anchors Have Outsized Consequences

2024
In August 2024, a modest quarter-point BOJ hike (from 0.1% to 0.25%) sent the yen surging and triggered a violent unwind of yen-funded trades — the Nikkei posted its worst single day since 1987, and US tech sold off in sympathy within 48 hours. Markets took roughly three weeks to fully stabilize once the panic selling ran its course.
The size of the rate move rarely matches the size of the unwind it can trigger.
1989
When the Bank of Japan raised rates five times between 1989 and 1990 to fight an asset bubble, the damage wasn't a single ugly session — it was slow. The bubble popped gradually, and Japan spent the next two decades in near-zero growth and deflation.
Sometimes tightening doesn't blow up markets in a day — it shows up years later, in growth that never fully comes back.
Directional Read

The variable to watch is whether Japan's exit from near-zero rates triggers real selling in yen-funded trades, or whether markets absorb it quietly the way today's flat session suggests. If the yen strengthens sharply and Japanese stocks wobble in the coming sessions, that stress will likely surface first in the same leveraged, AI-driven tech names currently leading US markets. Hold this one thought all week: today's calm was either the right call or the pause before the reaction — the yen will tell you which.

Scenario A — Quiet Absorption: The hike stays well-telegraphed and the yen holds steady — carry trades unwind gradually, and today's placid reaction turns out to have been the correct read.
Scenario B — Delayed Detonation: The yen spikes and Japanese equities sell off hard in the coming sessions, confirming a carry-trade unwind that drags down the same leveraged tech trade currently leading US markets.
