Today's Thesis

The Bond Market Is Doing the Panicking While Stocks Sit Still

US 30-year mortgage rates topped 7% for the first time in about 20 months, and the yield on the 30-year Treasury (what the government pays to borrow for three decades) hit its highest level since 2004. Stocks barely noticed: the S&P 500 closed at 7,704.13 (-0.02%), the Nasdaq was flat, and the Dow slipped 0.31%. The cause is the same one behind the past week's story. The Fed raised its rate on Sept. 16, oil is hovering around $100 because of the Iran war, and investors now doubt the Fed is finished. Mortgage rates follow long-term yields, not the Fed's own rate, and they have climbed more than a full percentage point since the war began. That means the pain is reaching households and governments through borrowing costs well before it reaches the stock index.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: a global bond selloff pushing borrowing costs up. Secondary: stretched households absorbing it.

BOND SELLOFF
Investors are demanding more to lend for long periods because they think inflation from oil will force more rate hikes.
The Fed's Sept. 16 hike was small, but long-term lenders are pricing what comes next. With oil near $100, they fear inflation stays hot and that central banks will keep raising rates. The worry is shared abroad: UK and Australian officials are signalling hikes, and warnings about heavy government debt in the US, France, the UK and Japan give lenders another reason to want higher pay for lending long. The 30-year Treasury yield at its highest since 2004 is what pushed mortgages past 7%.
This is not a one-day event. It lasts until oil falls convincingly or the Fed signals it is done, and neither has happened. Until then, assume borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and governments stay elevated, and don't count on a quick refinancing window.
STRETCHED HOUSEHOLDS
Higher rates are landing on borrowers who were already behind.
New federal data show 9.3 million student-loan borrowers in default and 1.5 million more seriously delinquent, with wages and Social Security payments exposed to collection. Add 7% mortgages and $100 oil, and household budgets are being squeezed from three directions at once. This is signal, not noise, but it moves slowly and shows up in spending data before it shows up in stock prices.
Watch the next jobs and consumer spending reports. If they weaken while prices stay high, the stagflation worry (slow growth plus sticky inflation) becomes the main story, because the Fed cannot fix both at once.
The Core Dynamic

Higher borrowing costs shrink what everything is worth, and stocks haven't done the math yet

Think of a house. When the mortgage rate goes from 6% to 7%, the house hasn't changed, but the monthly payment on it jumps, so buyers can only pay less for it. Stocks work the same way: investors pay today for future profits, and a higher long-term yield makes those profits worth less right now. The bond market has repriced the cost of money; the stock market so far has not. This episode is harder than a normal rate scare because the inflation comes from a war-driven oil price that rate hikes cannot lower, and because governments are borrowing heavily, which pushes long-term yields up independently of the Fed.

Historical Precedent

When long-term yields spike while stocks stay calm, one of them eventually gives way

2023
Long-term yields surged through the summer and autumn of 2023, with the 10-year Treasury yield touching about 5% in October, a level not seen since 2007. The S&P 500 fell about 10% from the end of July to late October. The slide stopped in November, when yields reversed after the Treasury signalled it would borrow less in long-term bonds than feared and the Fed signalled it had stopped hiking. Stocks then rallied through year-end.
Stocks bottomed when yields peaked, so the 30-year yield matters more to your portfolio than the Fed's headline rate.
1987
Through 1987 the yield on the 30-year Treasury climbed from roughly 7.5% to above 10% as inflation fears grew and the dollar weakened. Stocks kept rising until August, then fell, and on Oct. 19 the Dow dropped 22.6% in a single day. The Fed responded by supplying cash to the financial system, and the market stabilised within months without a lasting recession.
A long, calm stretch for stocks during a bond selloff does not mean stocks are immune; it can mean the repricing is simply coming later and all at once.
Directional Read

The variable that matters is the 30-year Treasury yield, and behind it, the oil price. If long-term yields stop climbing, the stock market's calm is justified and mortgage rates should stabilise near 7%. If they keep climbing while stocks hold steady, the gap between the two markets is the risk. Hold this all week: bonds set the price of money, and stocks eventually have to agree with them.

Scenario A — Yields plateau: Oil settles below $100 and the 30-year yield stops making new highs, so mortgage rates hold near 7% and stocks can stay near records.
Scenario B — Stocks catch down: The 30-year yield keeps setting new highs while a second Fed official argues for more hikes, and the Nasdaq leaders finally fall to meet the laggards.
