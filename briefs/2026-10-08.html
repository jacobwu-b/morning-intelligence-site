Today's Thesis

Oil Jumped 4% Again, but the Bond Market Didn't Follow It Higher

Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, jumped 4.07% to $104.28 after attacks on tankers in Middle East shipping lanes, a storm threatening US Gulf of Mexico output, and mixed signals from Washington on whether more strikes on Iran are coming. The Nasdaq fell 1.25% and the S&P 500 fell 0.47%, while the Dow edged up 0.1% and small caps were flat. The surprise is in the bond market. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 5.23% instead of rising with oil, which means today's tech selling was not the high-interest-rate squeeze of recent days. Oil is now doing the damage on its own, and the open question is whether bonds will keep ignoring it.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: a supply scare in oil. Secondary: bonds refusing to follow oil higher

OIL SUPPLY SCARE
Three separate threats to supply hit at once, and Brent got within a dollar of $105.
Attacks on tankers cut into shipping traffic, a storm approaching the Gulf of Mexico threatened US offshore production, and the White House asking for options on more Iran strikes raised the odds of a wider conflict. The price swung through the day as Trump's comments on Iran talks briefly eased worries, then faded. Brent closed at $104.28 and WTI at $91.49, so oil is sitting well above the $95 level that would signal real relief.
Expect oil to trade on headlines until Gulf flows visibly rebuild. With the war unsettled and storm season in play, the path of least resistance stays at $100 or above, which means energy costs keep leaking into inflation and keep the Fed's hand tied.
BONDS DIDN'T FOLLOW
Oil rose and Treasury yields fell, which is the opposite of the usual inflation reflex.
The 10-year yield slipped 0.05 points to 5.23% and the 30-year to 5.61%. The most likely reading is that bond buyers are treating this oil jump as a threat to growth and a reason to seek safety, not only as an inflation threat. That fits a Nasdaq that fell even as borrowing costs eased, and a fear gauge (the VIX) that edged up to 15.41 but stayed calm.
If oil stays above $104 and the 10-year climbs back above yesterday's 5.28%, the inflation worry is back in charge and stocks have a harder road. If yields keep sliding while oil rises, the market is pricing a slowdown, and that hurts earnings instead.
The Core Dynamic

Oil is a tax on everything, and this time there's no central-bank rescue

Think of a household whose heating and gas bill suddenly jumps 4% while the mortgage payment is already at its ceiling. Higher oil takes money out of every consumer and business before they spend a dollar on anything else. It also lifts the prices the Fed is trying to push down, so the usual relief valve of lower interest rates is unavailable. Normally an oil shock arrives when the central bank can ease the pain; today the Fed is leaning toward another hike, so the squeeze comes with no cushion, and that is what makes this version worse.

Historical Precedent

Oil shocks matter for as long as the barrels stay missing, not for how loud the first day was

2019
In September 2019, drone and missile strikes knocked out roughly half of Saudi Arabia's oil output. Brent jumped about 15% in a single day, its biggest one-day gain in decades. Saudi Arabia restored production within weeks, the price gave back nearly all of the spike, and the stock market barely noticed.
A supply shock fades as fast as the missing barrels come back, so the market is really pricing how long they stay missing.
2022
After Russia invaded Ukraine, Brent spiked above $120 in March 2022 and, because Russian supply stayed disrupted for months, spent most of the spring and summer above $100. With the Fed raising rates into that inflation, the S&P 500 fell into a bear market (a drop of 20% or more) by June.
When oil stays high and the central bank is tightening, stocks get squeezed from both sides, and the damage compounds.
Directional Read

The single variable that decides what happens next is whether Treasury yields rejoin oil on the way up. If they do, the inflation-and-Fed channel takes over and today's tech weakness becomes the start of something bigger. If yields keep falling while oil rises, the market is pricing a slowdown, which is a gentler path for the bond market but a worse one for company earnings. Watch the bonds, not the oil headline, because the bond market's reaction to $105 oil tells you which problem you actually have.

Scenario A — Supply returns, oil cools: Tanker traffic recovers and Brent closes back below $95 on two straight days, easing the pressure on the Fed and letting the S&P 500 push through its 7,818.93 record.
Scenario B — Oil and yields rise together: Brent closes above $110 while the 10-year yield returns above 5.28%, putting inflation and growth worries on the market at once and pushing the S&P 500 toward 7,700.
