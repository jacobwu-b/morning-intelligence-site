Today's Thesis

Washington Wants the Fed to Look Away From Oil. The Bond Market Isn't Listening.

Borrowing costs rose again: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed 0.05 points to 5.15% and the 30-year to 5.48%, and that is why the Nasdaq fell 0.91% while the Dow edged up 0.1%. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned the Fed not to raise rates into a war-driven energy shock, but pricing in the rate-futures market still shows a December hike as likely, so bond investors are siding with the inflation worry over the political plea. Tech takes the damage first because its profits sit far in the future, and the value of distant profits shrinks as yields rise. The same squeeze is already visible in housing, where home sales hit a 16-year low, so expect rate-sensitive stocks and housing to keep lagging unless oil breaks lower.

What's Actually Driving This

Rate-hike bets are driving yields higher (primary); a frozen housing market shows the cost (secondary)

HIKE BETS
Investors are pricing a December Fed hike despite Treasury's public objection, and they are demanding higher yields to lend.
The 10-year yield rose to 5.15% and the 30-year to 5.48%, with the dollar firming to 103.41. Higher yields hit the Nasdaq hardest (-0.91%, against -0.31% for small caps) because they shrink what far-off profits are worth today. The Dow's +0.1% suggests money is rotating toward steadier earners instead of leaving stocks altogether.
This pressure lasts as long as Brent stays above $95 and December hike odds stay high; a 10-year close back under 5.00% would be the first real sign it is loosening, and until then growth stocks are the weak spot.
HOUSING FREEZE
Mortgage rates near a three-year high have pushed home sales to a 16-year low.
Home sales fell to 3.75 million in October, down 8.8% from a year earlier, because long-term yields set the price of mortgages. This is the clearest sign that higher rates are hurting the real economy before the Fed has even hiked again. It is also the strongest evidence for Bessent's case that more tightening on top of an oil shock risks breaking something.
This is real and will persist while the 30-year yield stays near 5.5%; the thing to watch is whether the Fed treats housing weakness as a reason to pause or as proof that its policy is working.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed Is Being Asked to Fix an Oil Problem With a Demand Tool

Raising rates to fight an oil-driven price jump is like a family cutting its grocery spending after the store raises prices: it spends less, but the prices themselves don't move. The Fed's worry is that expensive fuel leaks into wages and everyday prices and becomes self-sustaining, so it may hike to stop a one-time shock from turning into a habit, and Treasury argues the cure costs more than the disease. What makes this episode different from a normal hiking cycle is the starting point: housing is already at a 16-year low, so the economic damage arrives sooner and the inflation relief is smaller, which raises the odds of an overshoot.

Historical Precedent

Hiking Into an Energy Shock Has Gone Two Different Ways

2008
In July 2008 the European Central Bank raised its key rate to 4.25% to fight inflation fed by oil near its $147 peak. Within three months, after Lehman Brothers collapsed, it joined a coordinated round of rate cuts, and oil fell below $50 by December.
A central bank that hikes against a price shock it can't control often ends up reversing within months once the demand damage shows.
1990
After Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990, oil roughly doubled to about $40 a barrel by October. The Fed under Alan Greenspan did not tighten; it kept cutting rates through the shock as the economy slid into recession, and oil fell back as the Gulf war ended in early 1991.
Looking through a supply shock works when people trust that inflation will come back down, and that trust is exactly what is being tested now.
Directional Read

The variable that decides this is oil: Brent holding above $95 keeps the hike case alive and pushes yields toward 5.25%, while a slide back under $90 would take the air out of December hike bets and give growth stocks room. Yields are the transmission line, so watch the 10-year more than the stock indexes. Housing is already weak, so each further rise in borrowing costs buys less inflation relief and costs more growth.

Scenario A — Oil breaks lower: Brent falls back under $90 as shipping and the waiver ease supply, the 10-year slips under 5.00%, and December hike odds fade.
Scenario B — Oil pushes toward $100: More strikes lift Brent toward $100, the 10-year reaches 5.25%, and the Nasdaq loses the 25,000 line as a December hike becomes the consensus.
