Today's Thesis

The Fed Hiked and Stocks Barely Blinked — the Dollar and Gold Did the Reacting

The S&P 500 finished flat at 7,764.64 while the Nasdaq rose 0.45% and the Dow fell 0.36%. A week after the Fed lifted its policy rate from 3.75% to 4.00%, the pain is showing up in currency and commodity markets rather than in the headline stock index. A stronger dollar pushed gold lower again because gold pays no interest, so every rate hike makes holding it relatively less attractive. The flat index hides a split underneath: the big technology leaders are holding up and the broader, more rate-sensitive stocks are not. That means this hike is landing on the part of the market that was already weakest.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: a Fed that has started hiking again. Secondary: oil slipping below $100, the one thing that could talk it out of more.

FED HIKING AGAIN
The Fed raised rates to 4.00%, and the market's reaction is showing up in the dollar and gold rather than in stocks.
Warsh's Fed raised rates a quarter-point on Sept. 16, and Collins has now said it will help return inflation to target. Investors are debating further increases, which lifts the dollar and pushes down gold, since gold pays no interest and is priced in dollars. Stocks absorbed it with an index-level shrug, but the Dow's decline against the Nasdaq's gain suggests the cost is landing on the broad middle of the market.
This is not settled in a day. The practical bet is that a rate cut this year is off the table, and that more hikes depend on oil and inflation data. Watch whether other Fed officials echo Collins; if they do, the dollar keeps climbing and gold stays under pressure.
OIL UNDER $100
Oil falling below $100 a barrel is the strongest argument against a second hike.
Energy feeds directly into prices for gasoline, shipping and almost everything else, so a sustained drop takes pressure off inflation. One day below a round number is a signal worth noting, not yet a trend. The UK shows the other side of the ledger: borrowing hit £18bn in August, tax rises look likely in next month's budget, and investors are openly testing how much more debt the government can pile on. High rates are squeezing indebted governments as well as indebted companies.
If oil stays below $100 and the next inflation readings cool, hike expectations fade and the dollar's rally loses its fuel. If oil pops back above $100, the Fed's job gets harder and the case for a second hike strengthens.
The Core Dynamic

Higher rates are a rising tide that only the biggest, richest companies can float above

Think of a town where the water is rising: houses on the hill stay dry, while everything on low ground gets wet first. The hill is a handful of giant technology companies that fund themselves from their own cash. The low ground is the roughly 60% of S&P 500 stocks already down more than 20% from their highs. Each rate hike raises the cost of borrowing for the companies with the least cushion, while the index stays high because the biggest names carry it. This instance is harder than usual because the index level gives no warning, and a stumble in a few leaders would have nothing underneath to catch it.

Historical Precedent

Central banks that hiked into an energy-driven inflation scare ended in two very different places

1994
The Fed began raising rates in February 1994 and doubled them from 3% to 6% over roughly a year. Bonds had one of their worst years in decades and stocks finished 1994 roughly flat. By 1995 the Fed had cooled inflation without a recession, and the S&P 500 returned about 37%.
A decisive hiking cycle can hurt for a year and still end in a soft landing, but the market goes sideways while it plays out.
2011
The European Central Bank raised rates in April and again in July 2011 (from 1% to 1.50%) while oil was elevated, treating an energy spike as an inflation problem. By November and December it had cut back to 1% as debt stress in Europe's governments spread and growth faltered.
Hiking into an energy price spike can be reversed within months when government debt stress takes over from inflation as the bigger danger.
Directional Read

The variable that matters most now is oil, because it decides whether this hike is the last or the first of several. Oil holding below $100 lets inflation fade and the dollar ease; oil back above $100 keeps the Fed's hand on the lever. Hold this all week: the stock index is calm, but the dollar and gold show what the market really thinks about the Fed.

Scenario A — Hike was enough: Oil stays under $100, inflation readings cool, the dollar stops climbing and gold steadies, which gives the beaten-down 60% of stocks room to catch up.
Scenario B — Hike was just the start: Oil climbs back above $100 or inflation data runs hot, more Fed officials sound like Collins, and the Nasdaq leaders that hold the index up finally sell off.
