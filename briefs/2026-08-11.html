Today's Thesis

The Iran Standoff Just Turned From Words to Weapons

A U.S. helicopter reportedly fired a missile at the rudder of a container ship trying to slip past the blockade off Pakistan — this is the first reported use of force against a vessel in this standoff, not just another round of hardened rhetoric. Oil pushed back toward $90 a barrel on the news, and Trump piling on a new demand for compensation from Iran closed off one more path to a quick deal. All three major indexes fell — the S&P 500 down 0.32%, the Nasdaq down 0.6% — with growth stocks taking the harder hit because a rising oil bill squeezes future profits more than it squeezes today's. This is the second straight session tech has underperformed the Dow, which is exactly the pattern that turns a one-day scare into a real rotation.

What's Actually Driving This

A missile strike on a blockade-running ship, and a Fed president arguing for hikes, not cuts

SHIP STRIKE ESCALATION
A U.S. helicopter reportedly fired on a container ship's rudder to stop it evading the blockade — that's a weapon, not a warning.
Until today, this 118-day standoff has run mostly on rhetoric — sanctions, demands, dueling statements. A reported kinetic strike on a vessel, stacked with Trump's new demand that Iran pay compensation for prior damage, is a different order of escalation, and it landed the same day oil pushed back toward $90 a barrel and yesterday's Hormuz-deal optimism kept unwinding.
This doesn't resolve in a day. Watch whether Iran responds directly to the strike — a retaliatory move against a ship, a tanker, or a military asset would confirm this has crossed from blockade enforcement into open conflict, and that's the scenario that sends oil through $95 and keeps this brief's risk-off tone in place for weeks, not days.
FED PUSHES BACK
A sitting Fed president just said rates aren't even restrictive yet — directly undercutting the market's rate-cut bet.
Hammack's comment is one voice, and one dissent doesn't set policy — that's the noise case. The signal case is that it reinforces a pattern already in motion: a Fed under political pressure to cut fast, courtesy of last week's weak jobs report and the renewed push to remove Governor Cook, is now facing its own officials arguing publicly for the opposite.
Watch whether other regional Fed presidents echo Hammack's hawkish line in the coming days versus citing the jobs weakness to argue for cuts. A visible split inside the committee — not just one dissent — is what would tell you this is a real credibility problem for the Fed rather than one contrarian voice.
The Core Dynamic

Oil is now the swing factor, not the Fed statement.

Every dollar oil adds back squeezes growth and adds an inflation input at the same time — that's the trap that makes a Fed cut look riskier the longer this standoff runs, regardless of what any single official says. It's why the market's reaction to today's strike ran through oil and gold first, before it ever touched Fed language.

Directional Read

The one variable that matters this week is oil's direction, and it now hinges on whether Iran retaliates or absorbs today's strike quietly. If oil holds near $90 and grinds higher, the market has to start pricing a real supply shock on top of an already murky rate-cut path; if the strike proves to be a one-off enforcement action and oil rolls back, this week's selloff is a blip.

Scenario A — Contained Enforcement: Iran absorbs the strike without retaliating, Hormuz talks quietly resume, and Brent gives back its recent gain — the selloff proves temporary.
Scenario B — Retaliation Spiral: Iran responds directly against a vessel or military target, oil pushes decisively through $92-95, and stocks keep bleeding as the Fed's cut case gets harder to make.
