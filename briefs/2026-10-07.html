Today's Thesis

The Fed Just Signaled Rates Aren't Done Rising, and Small Companies Took the Hit

Stocks slipped, with the S&P 500 down 0.22% to 7,801.77, just under yesterday's record. The damage was concentrated in small caps: the Russell 2000 fell 1.31%, six times as much as the Nasdaq. The cause is the Fed's September meeting minutes. Officials were unanimous that rates needed to go higher, and most expect another rate increase this year. That keeps the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.28%, a level that squeezes smaller companies, which borrow more and have fewer ways to absorb the cost. The market is now deciding whether to believe the Fed or the Treasury Secretary, who says yields fall once the Iran war ends.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: the Fed signals more rate hikes. Secondary: attacks stall the return of Gulf oil.

FED HIKE SIGNAL
The Fed's own minutes say the rate-hiking job isn't finished, and the market can no longer count on relief this year.
Officials were unanimous in September that rates needed to be higher to fight persistent inflation, and most now expect at least one more increase before year-end. That cuts directly against Treasury Secretary Bessent's message that inflation and yields will drop once the Iran war ends. The Fed is not waiting for the war to end. The 10-year yield barely moved (+0.01 points to 5.28%) and the 30-year rose 0.02 points to 5.66%, so the bond market had already priced much of this. The pain landed in small caps (-1.31%), which borrow more and are first to feel higher rates. The dollar rose 0.4% and gold fell 1.11%. Gold pays no interest, so higher yields and a stronger dollar make it less attractive.
Until inflation readings cool or oil falls decisively, another hike is the base case. Bet against any story that depends on rate relief before year-end, and expect small caps to stay the weak spot.
HORMUZ SETBACK
Attacks on shipping have cut traffic through the Strait of Hormuz to its lowest in over two months, stalling the oil recovery the inflation outlook depends on.
Hormuz is the narrow Gulf shipping lane that carries a large share of the world's oil. Brent held at $100.20 even as WTI, the US benchmark, fell 1.3% to $88.28. The gap between them widened to about $12 from about $11 yesterday. That most likely means the shortage is in seaborne, international supply, which US-produced oil does not fix. Fighting in Yemen and an attack on Riyadh's airport claimed by the Houthis add to the risk. Without more oil moving, the inflation the Fed is fighting does not ease.
If Hormuz traffic rebuilds and Brent closes below $95 two sessions in a row, the Fed's case for another hike weakens. If transits keep falling, oil stays near $100 and so does the hike risk.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed is running the air conditioning while the war keeps the furnace on

Think of a house where the furnace is stuck on and the thermostat controls only the air conditioner. The war keeps pushing energy costs up, and the Fed can only cool things by raising rates, which makes borrowing more expensive for everyone. Rate hikes cannot produce a single barrel of oil, so they work by weakening demand, and the weakest borrowers feel it first. That is why small caps fell 1.31% while the large AI-driven names barely moved. Usually the Fed looks past a one-time energy price jump. This time inflation was already persistent before the shock, so the Fed is not looking past it, and the pressure on fragile borrowers is greater than in a typical oil spike.

Historical Precedent

Central banks that raise rates into an energy shock have often had to reverse course

2008
The European Central Bank raised its rate to 4.25% in July 2008 to fight energy-driven inflation, just as Brent crude was peaking near $147. Within months the global financial crisis crushed demand, oil fell to roughly $40 by year-end, and the ECB began cutting in October.
Raising rates against an energy shock can look right on the day you do it and turn out to be wrong when demand cracks.
2011
The ECB raised rates in April and July 2011 as Brent held above $100, again citing oil-driven inflation. The eurozone debt crisis worsened and the bank reversed both hikes by December, returning to 1.0%.
A hike aimed at supply-driven inflation can end up tightening into a slowdown that the hiking central bank didn't see coming.
Directional Read

The variable that decides what happens next is oil, specifically whether Hormuz traffic recovers enough to pull Brent under $95. If it does, inflation pressure eases and the Fed's hike becomes less certain, which would also help small caps. If it doesn't, the Fed keeps its hike on the table, yields stay above 5.25%, and the AI rally has to carry a market that is narrowing underneath it. The Fed has told you its view: don't plan on lower rates this year.

Scenario A — Oil recovery resumes: Hormuz traffic rebuilds, Brent closes below $95 for two sessions, and the case for another Fed hike weakens, which lets small caps recover.
Scenario B — Yields push higher: Shipping attacks keep Brent near $100, the 10-year yield moves toward 5.50%, and small caps break below 2,700 while the AI-led indexes lose their cushion.
