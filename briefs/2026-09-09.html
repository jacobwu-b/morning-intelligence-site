Today's Thesis

The Strait Goes Quiet, and Washington's Bond Fix Gets Ignored

Two things broke today that matter more than the headline stock declines: tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway most Gulf oil tankers must pass through — has collapsed to single digits, and the Treasury's attempt to calm the bond market with a $6 billion buyback (the government repurchasing its own bonds to prop up prices and push yields down) got ignored, with yields climbing to their highest since the 2008 financial crisis. Both are escalations of stories you already know: oil above $100 a barrel from the widening Iran war, and a bond selloff that's been building for months. What's new is that oil just went from a fear premium to an actual supply problem, and the bond market just told Washington its usual tools don't work here. The Dow fell 0.77%, the S&P 0.48%, and the Nasdaq 0.64% — tech again losing less than the rest of the market, extending a pattern worth watching.

What's Actually Driving This

Oil turns into a physical supply shock while the bond market shrugs off Washington's fix

HORMUZ GOES QUIET
Tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen to single digits — this is now a supply story, not just a price story.
For months, oil's climb above $100 has been driven mostly by fear — traders pricing in the risk that the Iran war could disrupt shipping. Today's data shows actual tanker traffic through the strait has collapsed, alongside a strike that damaged US aircraft at a Jordan air base and a fresh warning from Trump to Iran over activity at a site called Pickaxe Mountain. That's the difference between markets pricing a risk and markets pricing a fact.
This doesn't resolve until either tanker traffic through Hormuz starts recovering, or the war widens further and the disruption becomes structural. Two sessions of Brent above $100 are on the board; a third would confirm a real floor under oil, not a spike.
TREASURY LOSES THE ROOM
The Treasury tried to buy back debt to calm bond yields, and the market ignored it — yields hit their highest since 2008 anyway.
A buyback is the government repurchasing its own bonds to shrink supply and push prices up, yields down — a standard tool to calm a jittery bond market. When yields keep rising right through that tool, it tells you the selloff isn't about supply or plumbing; it's about a fundamental fear, most likely the same oil-driven inflation story hitting stocks today. That's the signal version of this story, not the noise version — a policy tool failing usually means the market thinks the underlying problem is bigger than the tool.
Watch whether the next Treasury or UK gilt auction shows the same rejection or instead finds real demand — that will tell you if today was a one-off or the new pattern.
The Core Dynamic

Fear premiums and physical shortages behave differently — and oil just crossed that line.

A fear premium fades the moment tensions cool; a physical shortage doesn't fade until the actual flow of oil resumes, regardless of the news cycle. Hormuz traffic dropping to single digits means the second kind of problem is now in play, which is exactly why this oil spike is harder to trade around than the ones earlier in the war.

Directional Read

The variable that decides this week is simple: does tanker traffic through Hormuz recover, or does it stay frozen? If it recovers, oil's premium fades, the inflation scare cools, and the Fed keeps room to eventually cut. If it stays frozen, this becomes a genuine supply shortage that forces the Fed to hold rates high no matter what growth data says — and bond yields likely keep climbing with it. Hold this one thought all week: Hormuz answers oil, the Fed, and bonds all at once.

Scenario A — Hormuz Reopens: Tanker traffic data shows a rebound from single digits and Brent slips back under $95, and the inflation scare fades along with the pressure on the Fed.
Scenario B — The Shortage Sticks: Hormuz traffic stays frozen and Brent holds above $100 for a third straight session, confirming a real supply shortage that locks the Fed into holding rates high.
