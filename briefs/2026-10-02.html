Today's Thesis

Stocks Cheered a Dismal Jobs Report, Because Bad News Just Bought Them a Fed Pause

The US added just 29,000 jobs in September against roughly 90,000 expected, and unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1%. Yet the S&P 500 gained 0.73% to 7,722.72 and the Nasdaq jumped 1.19% to a record 27,191. Stocks rose because the market's biggest fear right now is not a weak economy. It is an inflation surge that could force the Fed to raise interest rates again. A soft jobs number makes a hike this month harder to justify, bond yields dropped, and growth and tech stocks, which suffer most when money is expensive, rallied. This is relief, not good news: the inflation problem is untouched, and the market is now betting that weak hiring will keep the Fed's hand still.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: a jobs miss that eased rate-hike fears. Secondary: a Nasdaq record that depends on falling yields.

BAD NEWS, GOOD TAPE
A weak jobs report weakened the case for a Fed rate hike, and the market treated that as a gift.
With inflation surging, the Fed has been under pressure to raise rates, and strong hiring would have given it cover to do so. Instead September came in at about a third of what economists expected, with unemployment up a notch. That is the first real crack in the argument for a hike this month. The crack is narrow, though. Layoffs have fallen and jobless claims sit near 57-year lows, so companies are holding staff but not adding any. That is a stall, not a collapse, and a stall does not push the Fed to cut.
This buys a pause, not a pivot. One soft month makes a hike harder to defend, but with inflation still surging a rate cut is not on the table, so don't read today as the start of an easing cycle. If next month's hiring rebounds, today's relief disappears quickly.
NASDAQ RECORD
Tech hit a record high because falling yields make future profits worth more today, not because anything changed at the companies.
Growth stocks are valued on earnings far in the future, and lower bond yields raise what those earnings are worth now. That is why the Nasdaq (+1.19%) outran the Dow (+0.49%) on a day with no corporate news behind it. One detail from the jobs report cuts both ways: data centers and healthcare were bright spots, while white-collar sectors kept struggling. That is the AI buildout creating construction and infrastructure work even as office hiring stays weak. Tesla's third-quarter deliveries (486,532, down 2% but well above the roughly 461,000 expected) added a little cheer, but it was a sideshow.
Treat the record as a yields trade until proven otherwise. If the Nasdaq holds its gains on a day when yields tick back up, that is real strength; if it gives them back the moment yields rise, today was relief, not conviction.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed is treating a fever and a weak pulse at the same time

Picture a doctor whose patient has a high fever (inflation) and a faltering pulse (hiring). The fever medicine, higher rates, risks weakening the pulse further, and the pulse medicine, lower rates, risks making the fever worse. Today the market cheered because the doctor may hold off on the fever medicine, but the fever hasn't broken. This is harder than a normal standoff because the Fed controls only short-term rates. The global bond sell-off that pushed US borrowing costs to their highest since 2002 is partly about government deficits, so even a Fed pause may not bring long-term borrowing costs down much.

Historical Precedent

Markets have cheered weak data in an inflation fight before. It lasted exactly until the Fed said no.

2022
From mid-June to mid-August 2022, the S&P 500 rallied roughly 17% on hopes that slowing data would make the Fed ease off its rate hikes while inflation was still high. Then Fed Chair Powell's short, blunt speech in late August said rates would stay high for as long as needed. The S&P fell more than 3% that day and made new lows by October.
A market that rallies on bad news is betting on the Fed's mercy, and the Fed only has to say no once.
1990
After Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990, oil prices roughly doubled and a US recession had already begun that summer. Job losses mounted over the following months, and the Fed ultimately cut its policy rate from about 8% to 3% by 1992, setting aside inflation worries as the labor market kept weakening.
The Fed turns when jobs weaken month after month, not when one report disappoints.
Directional Read

The variable that matters is which worry the Fed weights more: inflation or jobs. If inflation cools while hiring stays soft, the Fed can hold and stocks keep their footing; if inflation stays hot against a weakening job market, the market loses its cushion. Today's rally priced the first outcome. Hold this all week: bad news is only good news until inflation says otherwise.

Scenario A — Inflation cools, Fed holds: The next inflation report shows price growth easing while hiring stays soft, the Fed leaves rates alone, yields keep falling, and the Nasdaq extends its record.
Scenario B — Stagflation takes hold: Inflation re-accelerates while hiring stays weak, hike risk comes back, and stocks fall on bad jobs news because it now signals a squeeze rather than a pause.

Watch Next Week
The Fed raises rates at its meeting this month. — It would mean inflation outweighed the jobs miss, and today's rally priced the wrong outcome.
The next unemployment reading comes in at 4.3% or higher. — Two straight upticks would suggest a real softening rather than a one-month wobble, moving the Fed debate toward growth.
The next consumer price report shows annual inflation higher than the prior reading. — Weak jobs with accelerating prices is the stagflation squeeze, and the market's bad-news-is-good-news logic would break.
Fed officials publicly say the September jobs miss does not change the case for raising rates. — It would show the hike is still live and that today's relief rally rests on a hope the Fed isn't sharing.
