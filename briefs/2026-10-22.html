Today's Thesis

Tech Sold Off Hard While Treasuries Rallied — This Time It's the Lenders, Not the Rates

The Nasdaq fell 2.6% to 26,009 and the S&P 500 lost 1.21%, yet the Dow was flat and small caps (the Russell 2000) edged up 0.11%. That is a sell-off in big tech, not in the whole market. Interest rates are not the cause: the 10-year Treasury yield fell 0.19 points to 4.95%, so money ran into safe government bonds instead of away from them. The most likely cause is the worry over how heavily AI spending is funded with borrowed money, which is hard to square with SpaceX preparing to borrow roughly another $40 billion for Nvidia chips while it explores a combination with Tesla. Oil is making it harder to fix, with Brent up 4% to $113.17 on Houthi-Saudi attacks and a UAE oil spill.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: lenders get nervous about AI borrowing. Secondary: oil surges and blocks the usual Fed rescue.

AI DEBT WORRY
Tech fell 2.6% because the problem is who pays for AI spending, not what money costs.
Yields fell and the Nasdaq still dropped, which rules out higher rates as the cause. SpaceX is about to borrow roughly another $40 billion to buy Nvidia chips while it and Tesla form special committees to consider combining. That ties one of the most indebted AI spenders to a Nasdaq heavyweight. The Dow was flat and small caps rose, so the damage sits where the AI borrowing sits. Gold fell 2.16% too, which suggests investors were selling winners to raise cash rather than hiding in familiar havens.
The pressure lasts until lenders show they will keep funding AI projects on similar terms. If SpaceX gets its $40 billion without a visible price jump, today fades within days; if the cost jumps, expect the Nasdaq to test 25,500.
OIL SURGE
Brent jumped 4% to $113.17, which means inflation pressure is still building.
Houthi-Saudi attacks and a UAE oil spill hit the global market, while Washington invoked the Cold War-era Defense Production Act to push US oil output and refining. That move signals that officials see supply as dangerously tight. The widening Brent-WTI gap, now $13.70, shows the shortage is worst in the global market. Higher fuel costs feed the 3.7% inflation the Fed is already fighting.
Watch whether Brent holds above $110 over the next few sessions; a close at $120 or above would mean the oil shock is overpowering the bond rally and the Fed's room to ease.
The Core Dynamic

A Credit-Fueled Boom Meets a Fed That Can't Rescue It

Think of a friend expanding his restaurant chain entirely on borrowed money. It works fine while the bank keeps saying yes, and the real risk is the bank's mood, not the debt. AI companies are spending ahead of their revenue, and much of that spending goes to chipmakers, so if lenders hesitate, the spending slows and the chipmakers' sales shrink, which scares lenders further. Normally the Fed would cut rates to calm lenders. This time oil at $113 and 3.7% inflation have the Fed leaning toward a hike, so the usual rescue is off the table, and that makes this episode more dangerous than a standard credit scare.

Historical Precedent

When One Heavily Indebted Borrower Spooks Lenders, the Fed's Reaction Decides the Outcome

1970
Penn Central, then the largest US railroad, went bankrupt in June 1970 while owing a large amount of short-term IOUs (commercial paper) that lenders refused to roll over. Corporate borrowers everywhere suddenly found it hard to fund themselves. The Fed responded by telling banks its emergency lending window was open and relaxing limits on rates banks could pay for large deposits, and the funding panic faded.
A lender panic ends when the central bank makes clear it will lend, which is exactly the move today's Fed is poorly placed to make.
2015
Energy and mining companies had borrowed heavily at high interest rates, and when oil collapsed, a bond fund (Third Avenue) froze withdrawals in December 2015. The stress stayed largely inside those sectors, but stocks slid roughly 15% before bottoming in February 2016. The Fed had raised rates in December 2015 and then paused for a year, and oil stabilized.
Credit stress stays contained when the Fed stands down and the commodity pressure eases; today oil is rising and the Fed is leaning the wrong way.
Directional Read

The deciding variable is whether lenders keep funding AI borrowers at tolerable cost, with SpaceX's roughly $40 billion borrowing as the live test. If it clears smoothly, today looks like a one-day scare and the Nasdaq can retake 26,713. If lenders push back, the sell-off spreads, and with oil at $113 and the Fed leaning toward a hike, there is no cushion. A stock slide with bonds rallying and oil surging means the market fears a funding squeeze more than higher rates, and the Fed cannot easily fix that.

Scenario A — Lenders stay on board: SpaceX's borrowing prices without strain, Brent falls back below $110, and the Nasdaq climbs back above 26,713.
Scenario B — Funding squeeze spreads: AI borrowers face visibly higher borrowing costs, small caps join the sell-off, the VIX closes at 25 or higher, and the Nasdaq breaks below 25,500.
