Today's Thesis

AI's Lenders Just Blinked, and the Fed Chair Isn't Riding to the Rescue

Tech took the damage: the Nasdaq fell 2.15% and the S&P 500 lost 1.09%, while the Dow slipped just 0.34%. The VIX (the market's fear gauge) jumped 21% to 19.99. The trigger was not interest rates, because the 10-year yield barely moved at 5.10%. It was the cost of borrowing for AI infrastructure companies. CoreWeave, an AI data-center company, has seen its bond yields and its credit default swaps (insurance contracts against a borrower failing to pay) surge this week as its stock sold off, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh warned the same day that AI spending itself could deliver an inflation shock. Crude oil rose another 3.7% on top of that, so the market is now asking whether the AI build-out can keep getting funded when the Fed is not in a mood to help.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: AI borrowers' funding costs spike and the Fed chair calls AI spending inflationary. Secondary: oil pushes back above $100 WTI and $109 Brent.

AI FUNDING STRESS
The companies renting out AI computing power are suddenly being charged more to borrow, and stock investors are reading that as an early warning.
CoreWeave's bond yields and credit default swap prices surged this week as its shares sold off, which means lenders, not just stock traders, are demanding more protection. Warsh then added that a surge in AI spending could produce an inflation 'shock' and pledged to contain prices. That is a Fed chair telling AI companies that cheap money is not coming back to bail out their funding plans. Nasdaq fell 2.15%, worse than the Russell 2000's 1.66% drop, so this was a tech-led sell-off rather than a broad flight from small companies.
The sell-off lasts as long as lenders keep charging more; a calm in CoreWeave's bond and insurance prices would make this a one- or two-day shakeout, while further spikes would mean the most debt-dependent AI names stay the ones to be wary of.
OIL RE-ACCELERATES
Crude jumped nearly 4% as attacks around the Arabian Peninsula reminded the market that Gulf supply routes are not secure.
Brent closed at $109.86, just short of $110, and WTI at $101.41. Missiles hit Aden airport in Yemen and a missile headed for Yanbu, a Saudi Red Sea export port, was intercepted. Washington is also seeking swaps of up to 17 million barrels from its emergency stockpile, which is a small amount against world demand but shows that policymakers see the price as a problem. Households have little cushion: inflation-adjusted hourly pay has slipped back to about where it stood when Trump's second term began, so every fuel price jump lands on wallets that have already been squeezed.
A Brent close at or above $110 would confirm energy inflation is re-accelerating and make a Fed hike harder to avoid; a stockpile release that pushes Brent back under $100 would show the spike was a security scare.
The Core Dynamic

The AI boom is being funded on credit, and the lender of last resort is looking the other way

Picture a homeowner building a huge addition on a credit line, and halfway through the bank starts raising the rate and the city's top official says the construction is driving up local prices. Stocks price in future profits, but bonds price whether the company survives to earn them, so when lenders get nervous the equity cushion thins fast. Usually a credit scare ends when the Fed signals it will ease. This time the Fed chair is warning about inflation, oil is rising, and bonds did not even rally as a safe haven (the 10-year yield rose 0.01 points), so the usual rescue and the usual shock absorber are both missing.

Historical Precedent

Debt-funded booms end one of two ways: the central bank rescues the lenders, or nobody does

2001
Telecom companies borrowed heavily in the late 1990s to build fiber networks, and the Nasdaq peaked in March 2000. As lenders pulled back and borrowers began failing (Global Crossing filed for bankruptcy in January 2002), the Nasdaq kept falling until October 2002, roughly 78% below its peak. The Fed cut rates aggressively through 2001 and could not stop it.
Rate cuts can rescue borrowers who are short of cash, but they cannot rescue projects that never earn enough to pay their debt.
1998
Russia's default and the collapse of the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management froze credit markets, and the S&P 500 fell nearly 20% between July and early October. The Fed then cut rates three times in about seven weeks, credit reopened, and the Nasdaq nearly doubled during 1999.
A credit scare fades quickly when the central bank is free to cut, and today the Fed is debating a hike instead.
Directional Read

The variable that decides what comes next is whether the funding stress in CoreWeave's bonds and default insurance stays confined to the most indebted AI names or spreads to other AI borrowers. If it stays contained, this Nasdaq drop is a sharp shakeout inside a market that still has a floor, since the S&P 500 at 7,659 is still above 7,600. If it spreads, the Fed's inflation focus means no cavalry arrives and the tech decline has room to run. Watch the lenders, not the stock tickers.

Scenario A — Stress stays contained: CoreWeave's bond yields and default-insurance prices stop climbing and the VIX falls back below 18, which would make today a one-off shakeout in the most debt-heavy names.
Scenario B — Stress spreads: Brent closes at or above $110 while AI borrowers' funding costs keep rising, forcing a repricing of both inflation and AI spending plans and pushing the VIX well past 20.
