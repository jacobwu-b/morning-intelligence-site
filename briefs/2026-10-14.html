Today's Thesis

Hot Inflation Put a Fed Hike Back on the Table, and Tech Paid for It

Consumer prices rose 3.7% over the past year in September, up from 3.4% in August, and gasoline did most of the damage. That puts a Fed rate hike this month back in play. The Nasdaq fell 1.88% to 26,693 and the S&P 500 fell 1.11%, while the Dow barely moved (-0.09%). Growth stocks are priced on profits far in the future, and a 10-year Treasury paying 5.19% with no risk already gives investors a strong reason to sell them. A hike means that bar stays high for longer. Long-term bond yields slipped slightly instead of spiking, so this was a repricing of stock valuations, not a panic.

What's Actually Driving This

Primary: hot inflation revives hike risk. Secondary: oil that won't come down

HIKE RISK RETURNS
A 3.7% inflation print took a Fed rate hike this month from background risk to live possibility.
Inflation had been drifting up, but the September jump removed the argument that the Fed could simply wait. Stocks fell in the places most sensitive to the price of money: the Nasdaq and the Russell 2000 (small companies, which borrow heavily) both lost nearly 2%. Gold fell 1.66% as the dollar rose 0.36%, which is what rate-hike pricing usually does: gold pays no interest, so a higher rate makes it less attractive. The most likely reason long-term yields slipped is that investors see a hike as the Fed doing its job, not losing control. Shorter-term rate moves would confirm or refute that.
Expect the pressure on growth stocks to last until inflation cools or the Fed signals it will hold. Until then, bet against rate relief and expect the most expensive stocks to keep taking the hits.
STICKY ENERGY
Gasoline drove the inflation jump, and crude is not retreating.
Brent closed at $106.06 and WTI at $95.09, both higher on the day, so the fuel costs that lifted September prices have not eased. The Fed cannot lower oil prices; its only tool is slowing the rest of the economy to offset them, which is why energy inflation makes hikes more likely. Washington's consideration of easing sanctions on shadow-fleet tankers could trim shipping costs, but it is only under consideration.
A Brent close below $100 would be the first real sign that the inflation pressure is easing; a close at $110 or higher would make the next inflation report look worse than this one.
The Core Dynamic

The Fed can't fix an oil shock without hurting everything else

When prices rise because oil is expensive, the Fed cannot drill for more crude, so its only move is making borrowing costly enough that people spend less elsewhere. That makes every hot energy-driven inflation reading a threat to stock valuations, not just to your grocery bill. What is different now is that this tightening arrives with the 10-year already above 5% and stocks within about 1.3% of their record, so there is no cushion of cheap money to absorb it.

Directional Read

The variable that decides this is the 10-year Treasury yield. If it holds near 5.2% even as hike odds rise, stocks can stabilize because the pain is confined to valuations. If it pushes toward 5.40%, the sell-off will probably broaden beyond tech. A hot inflation number moved stocks more than it moved bonds, so watch the bond market for the signal that this is getting worse.

Scenario A — Yields hold, tech steadies: The 10-year stays near 5.2% and oil slips below $100, so the Nasdaq regains 27,000 and today reads as a one-day repricing.
Scenario B — Bonds join the sell-off: The 10-year pushes toward 5.40% and Brent heads for $110, so the sell-off spreads beyond tech and the VIX climbs through 20.
